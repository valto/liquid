# Liquid Software for finance and investment

Liquid Software treats software specialization as an investment in fulfilling a meaningful capability. The financial question is **which feasible strategy delivers the best cumulative net value, given demand, timing, uncertainty and the cost of changing course?** A low runtime price is one input to that comparison.

This perspective serves finance leaders, procurement teams and investors assessing implementation choices. It is a decision framework, rather than a valuation model or an investment recommendation.

## Compare strategies over the same horizon

Identify the accepted outcome and alternatives before estimating costs. An existing service, a manual process, direct intelligent execution, a reusable function and staged combinations may all be relevant. Quality, authority and required reliability constrain which choices are feasible.

Count benefits and costs consistently: initial enablement, development, assurance, recurring execution, supervision, adoption, upkeep and transitions. Compare the timing of accepted benefits as well as their size. If delays already reduce benefits in the model, do not deduct the same loss again as opportunity cost.

## Use break-even for the question it actually answers

The elementary threshold `N = F / (a − s)` compares fixed incremental software cost with a per-execution saving when `a > s`. With the supplied v3 assumptions of €1,200 fixed cost, €2.50 agent execution and €0.10 software execution, the threshold is 500 comparable executions.

This is an illustrative cost check. The investment depends on **remaining future demand**, not whether a historical counter has reached 500. Already-paid construction is sunk when assessing future operation. Different outcomes, timing, failure, maintenance or transition costs require a fuller comparison.

## Examine reversibility and adoption

A staged approach may deliver feasible value while uncertainty is resolved, then specialize selected work. Waiting can also cost value, and some controls must exist upfront. Flexibility is valuable when a plausible future change makes it useful; architectural elegance alone is not a benefit estimate.

An implementation can be deployed yet underused or fail to deliver a promised outcome. Reversing it may require a second transition and reconciliation. The paper distinguishes these mechanisms and source populations; it does not provide a universal software failure rate or a standard reversal multiplier.

## Value the transition and continuing support

The Continuous Intelligence companion gives familiar workflows and stable operations explicit value. A new final state can be attractive while its transition destroys part of the expected benefit. Include retraining, disruption, switching and absorption capacity in the comparison.

Parallel versions may preserve value for different cohorts, but add compatibility, security, assurance and upkeep. Compare release, adoption and retirement separately. The companion's change heuristic is qualitative; its categories belong once in the existing lifecycle model. Reduced tokens or feature adoption alone do not establish net value.

## Make uncertainty inspectable

Use observed costs, explicit estimates and scenario ranges. Record what would change the decision. Where probabilities are unsupported, ranges and stated unknowns are more informative than invented precision. Expected value does not make a path acceptable if it violates a hard constraint or creates unacceptable downside.

The optional decision record can structure this assessment. The framework's calculations illustrate reasoning; matched real capability comparisons are still needed to test its claims.
