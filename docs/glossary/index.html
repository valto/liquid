Glossary

Shared definitions distinguish information, meaning, authority, capabilities and their changing realizations.

Conceptual source: Valto Loikkanen · AI-assisted synthesis and research

Read source text ↗

Liquid Software: implementations that can emerge, adapt, stabilize and retire around actual needs, within durable meaning and authority.

Information-centric: preserving attributable information independently of one application or presentation.

Intelligence-native: treating interpretation and suitable fulfillment of emerging needs as an initial capability.

Adaptive interface: a presentation or interaction chosen around recipient, purpose, information and environment.

Source evidence: retained observations or records with attributable origin and relevant context.

Accepted assertion: a proposition accepted within an explicit authority and revision boundary; it is distinct from an unaccepted inference.

Derived structure: a rebuildable representation used for retrieval, interpretation or presentation. Its physical location does not confer canonical authority.

Capability: a meaningful ability defined through purpose, boundaries and accepted outcomes.

Realization: a current intelligent, deterministic or hybrid means of fulfilling a capability.

Principal: the individual or entity whose authority, information or interests are represented.

Operational actor: the human, agent or application initiating or fulfilling an interaction.

Degrees of decision freedom: contextual space for further choices within inherited decisions, commitments and resources.

Shared semantics: agreed meaning that makes an interaction interpretable; common syntax alone is insufficient.

Schema: a type or structural description used in an interaction. Its authorship, timing, lifetime and negotiation are distinct from validation under a particular current language.

MLE: Minimum Logical Element, the smallest unit that retains meaningful context across relevant perspectives.

CRD: Capability Requirements Document, an implementation-independent description of one contextually meaningful capability.

PIOS / EIOS: related proposals for personal and entity information, knowledge and continuity.

PAIX: Private AI Exchange, a related body of work on interaction across participants.

Capability economics: comparing feasible realizations through risk-adjusted cumulative net value over a relevant horizon, including full lifecycle costs and retained flexibility.

Time to value: elapsed time before a capability delivers an accepted useful outcome; its economic effect depends on the timing and value of those outcomes.

Execution continuum: reversible choices among direct intelligent execution, skills, functions, modules, applications and hybrids; not a required maturity sequence.

Double transition cost: the possible combined cost of adopting an unsuitable implementation and restoring or moving to another one; a scenario to assess, not a universal multiplier.

Maturity bias: in Economics of Execution v3, limits to perceived alternatives arising from current understanding and experience. The earlier v2-derived explanation emphasized the related mistake of judging decision quality by an implementation's position on a presumed progression. This is a source-attributed conceptual revision, not a validated assessment.

Informed selection: deliberately comparing recognized feasible alternatives and their limitations, supported where useful by practical experience. A conventional implementation can be the informed choice.

Understanding, experiential and implementation maturity: respectively, the operating alternatives a decision-maker recognizes, has practical experience with, and chooses to implement. V3 distinguishes them; visible implementation does not establish the other two.

Evidence-driven materialization: using demonstrated or credibly anticipated need to justify creating or stabilizing a specialized realization. Upfront requirements can justify investment before observed use.

Premature determinism: fixing behavior before relevant variation, exceptions and future change are sufficiently understood. It is distinct from enforcing intentional safety or authority constraints.

Capability-level optimization: comparing and revisiting realizations at meaningful units of purpose and contract, including reuse across activities and the cost of coordination.

Dissolution: retiring or replacing a realization while preserving necessary evidence and accepted commitments, or explicitly revising those commitments through proper authority.

These economic terms are developed from The Economics of Execution, version 2.0 and its version 3.0 expansion. V3's maturity spectrum is a conceptual classification; the separate named maturity companion was not supplied.
