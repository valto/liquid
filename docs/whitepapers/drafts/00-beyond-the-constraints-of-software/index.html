The transition toward an information-centric, intelligence-native world

Conceptual source and direction: Valto Loikkanen. Research and drafting: Codex (OpenAI), at his request. Research draft 1.0 · 26 November 2026.

Abstract

Software has enabled extraordinary progress while imposing a less visible constraint: people often must express their information, activities and ambitions in forms that existing applications can accommodate. Valto Loikkanen proposes reversing this relationship. Preserve information and its ownership over time; establish meaningful human decisions and boundaries; let intelligence interpret needs; and create, retain or retire software according to the value it provides. This paper introduces that synthesis and maps fifteen complementary papers. It argues for a change in architectural priorities, rather than a prediction that software, structure or institutions will disappear. Its contribution is a connected model of information continuity, adaptive interaction, evolving implementation and delegated human authority. [S01]

1. The constraint that became an organizing principle

Consider a small organization that wants to understand why a project changed direction. Its evidence may include an original conversation, a spreadsheet, an approval, a customer message and a later correction. One application preserves the spreadsheet, another the conversation, and another the approval. The question belongs to the organization, but answering it requires navigating the divisions imposed by its tools.

The same mismatch appears at several levels. A database records anticipated fields; a document selects a narrative; a dashboard selects indicators; an API exposes chosen operations; and an application admits a defined workflow. Each selection is useful. Trouble arises when the selection becomes the only surviving information or the only permitted way to use it.

The historical problem is therefore an inversion of priorities. Temporary choices about representation and execution become permanent constraints on information and human activity. Software is one contributor to this inversion, alongside institutions, incentives, physical limits and imperfect knowledge. Calling it a structural bottleneck identifies a mechanism to investigate, rather than a complete explanation of economic history. [S19]

2. The proposed reversal

Loikkanen's synthesis has four enduring concerns: whose interests are served, what information survives, what capabilities mean, and which decisions have already been made. Around these, interfaces and implementations can change at different speeds. [S01]

An information-centric system preserves attributable information independently of a particular presentation or application. An intelligence-native system can interpret emerging needs and select suitable ways to fulfill them. Liquid Software describes the changing implementations that support those needs. Degrees of Decision Freedom describe the space in which humans and their delegated actors may adapt without repeating decisions already settled at a higher level.

These concepts reinforce one another. A generated interface is useful when it can reach reliable information. A changing backend is sustainable when the capability retains meaning. An autonomous optimizer is acceptable when its authority is bounded. Ownership is practical when information and relationships survive the replacement of the current tool.

The separately supplied AI-assisted umbrella synthesis names four inversions across storage, retrieval, interfaces and software. Evidence is retained before anticipated questions narrow it; retrieval responds to new needs; interaction follows context; and implementations follow useful capability. This provides a parallel reading path through the same discussion, with its conceptual-source attribution and research limits preserved. [S30, pp. 2–4, 12]

3. Languages and formats are ways of interacting

The conversation's later clarification is essential: begin by treating interaction mechanisms as languages and formats through which a participant expresses a need or receives information. Natural language, a structured query, a diagram and a machine message can all play this role. The architectural question is what the interaction means, who it serves and how it can be fulfilled. [S01, S26]

Technical specifications remain useful for interoperability, but they need not dictate the full conceptual model. A schema may be generated from shared terminology, inferred under documented conventions, or negotiated for a particular exchange. Its existence does not imply a permanently designed application or database. Conversely, recognizable syntax alone does not establish shared meaning: two participants can use the same field name for different concepts.

The receiving system must resolve both meaning and authority. The requester contributes its need and desired representation; the receiver contributes available information, supported capabilities and disclosure boundaries. Intelligence can help reconcile these within previously established guidance. When it cannot, the system should expose the unresolved question rather than fabricate agreement.

4. A model with different rates of change

Concern What should persist What may adapt
Principals Identity, relationships and delegated authority Representatives and service providers
Information Originals, provenance and accepted revisions Indexes, interpretations and projections
Capabilities Purpose, boundaries and meaningful outcomes Agentic, hybrid and software realizations
Interaction Agreed meaning and applicable commitments Languages, formats and contextual presentation
Governance Human decisions and external obligations Operational choices within delegated freedom

Persistence is not immutability. A corrected decision or revised capability must be able to change deliberately, with its history preserved. Fluidity is likewise not continuous mutation: a stable implementation may remain the best choice for years.

PIOS and EIOS are relevant public framework proposals for the personal and entity information foundations. CRD supplies capability documentation; PAIX explores contact and communication across independent participants. They contextualize the synthesis without being mandatory components of every adoption. Their publications are framework evidence, not independent proof of the complete vision. [S07–S10]

5. The collection and its independent questions

Papers 01–03 examine the software bottleneck, intelligence as a starting capability, and human decision freedom. Papers 04–07 define Liquid Software, adaptive interfaces, preservation for unknown future value, and adaptive retrieval. Papers 08–12 develop the optimizer, emerging interfaces, capability documentation, principals and the software lifecycle. Papers 13–15 examine internet economics, the transition to information-centric computing, and a composable reference architecture.

Paper 00 supplies the intellectual entry point. Paper 14 examines how to change existing systems. Paper 15 describes how the components can interact. Keeping these purposes distinct allows a reader to use one paper without adopting the entire collection.

6. What changes economically and socially

If adaptation becomes inexpensive and reliable, previously uneconomic needs may become serviceable. A person could obtain a specialized tool without waiting for a mass-market product; an organization could reshape a workflow around its own information; and a supplier could provide a capability through several interaction formats.

These possibilities do not guarantee decentralization. Compute, distribution, trusted data and liability could become more concentrated even as interface generation becomes easier. The relevant question is whether users gain effective choice: can they change a provider while preserving their information, commitments and working capability?

Human decision speed may become a limiting factor in some workflows. The remedy proposed here is to make a prior decision usable across many later actions. This requires understandable delegation and enforceable boundaries, alongside opportunities for human revision. It is a governance hypothesis, not a reason to remove human involvement wherever software can act.

7. A transition that can be tested

A useful first experiment starts with one existing information need. Preserve its sources, define the capability, establish a bounded interaction and compare three ways of serving it: existing software, direct intelligent execution and a combination. Measure total effort, quality, latency, recovery and the ability to change the implementation without changing the meaning.

Then observe actual demand. If repetition justifies a specialized implementation, build it and test whether it preserves permissions, provenance and behavior. If demand declines, withdraw the implementation while retaining the promised capability or explicitly renegotiating its availability.

The synthesis would be weakened if adaptation consistently costs more than it saves, shared semantics fail across participants, human delegation becomes opaque, or source preservation makes systems unusable. These are central research questions, not peripheral implementation details.

8. Intellectual lineage and contribution

The collection acknowledges existing work on malleable software, local-first ownership, dynamic computational media, data independence and end-to-end correctness. It extends the conversation's account by connecting these concerns to capability continuity, evolving retrieval and operational delegation. [S04–S06, S18, S20, S21]

Valto Loikkanen is the source of the conceptual synthesis developed here. This attribution does not claim that every component or the term Liquid Software originated with him; JFrog's earlier use of that term concerns continuous software delivery. The present research draft offers an explicit, testable interpretation of his connected argument. [S03]
