                                                                  Abstract
           This paper develops a general architecture in which organizational intelligence can evolve continuously with‐
       out forcing operations, software interfaces, or users to change continuously. It connects recurring review cycles,
       event‐triggered learning, the independent value of stability, parallel release and phased retirement, personal‐
       ized experiences, capability‐level specifications, deterministic reasoning, and customer‐led outcome verification.
       The framework is conceptual: examples describe design opportunities rather than already implemented product
       features.

Executive synthesis
Liquid Software is not software that changes constantly. It is an architecture in which capabilities and experi‐
ences can change when there is sufficient reason, without unnecessarily disrupting what already works.
A traditional software product bundles capabilities, interface choices, release timing, and commercial packaging
into one vendor‐defined experience. A traditional organization similarly bundles observation, analysis, decisions,
and change into management meetings and fixed planning cycles. AI makes it possible to separate these things.
The critical question is not simply what can now be automated? It is what should be reviewed, when, why, at what
depth, and how should a justified change be introduced?
The answer must account for two truths at once:

   1. Continuous information processing and learning can improve decisions, lower costs, and detect changing
      needs earlier.
   2. Stability, familiarity, predictable processes, and continuity have positive economic and human value.
      Forced change can destroy part of the very value an improvement was intended to create.

This paper integrates six interdependent concepts developed in the discussion:

    • Layered review and intelligence cycles. Continuous, daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, and annual perspec‐
      tives differ in purpose and depth. Event‐triggered reviews supplement rather than abolish them.
    • A self‐improving meta‐loop. A separate learning capability evaluates whether reviews, triggers, thresholds,
      and decisions are effective, including whether the organization changes too much or too little.
    • Governed evolution through parallel versions. A new implementation can be released alongside an exist‐
      ing one, evaluated, optionally adopted, retained for a subset of users, or retired deliberately.
    • Individual, contextual experiences. Users should not be forced into the same feature set or interface.
      Focused, dense, hybrid, and agent‐only experiences can share a common capability layer.
    • Capability‐based execution. CRDs define durable capability meaning; Skills and agents orchestrate usage;
      code, deterministic rules, or compiled reasoning implement particular parts. Implementation can evolve
      without silently changing requirements.
    • Customer‐led value verification. Pulse CXM contributes the outside‐in loop connecting genuine customer
      signals to coordinated action and conclusions about whether the original customer problem has actually
      improved.

Unifying principle: Continuous intelligence. Adaptive evolution. Stable operation and experience.
The paper distinguishes established practices and third‐party claims from the proposed architecture. It is a design
thesis and research resource, not a claim that one existing product implements the entire model.

1. The underlying problem: valuable capabilities, unwanted change
1.1 The vendor’s product is not the individual’s working environment
The conventional product model serves many people through one product roadmap. New features are added
because they serve some customers, improve competitive positioning, satisfy enterprise buyers, or expand the
vendor’s revenue. Yet the additional capability often arrives in the user interface of everybody else, including
people who have no reason to use it. A feature may be valuable in aggregate while being distracting in a particular
person’s workflow.
In its 2019 Feature Adoption Report, Pendo analyzed three months of anonymized data across 615 Pendo sub‐
scriptions and reported that roughly 80% of features in the average product were rarely or never used; around
12% of features accounted for 80% of average daily usage volume [R7]. These are aggregate measurements of
the products in its sample, not proof that each individual uses only 20% or that 80% of every interface can safely
be removed.
The more important architectural question is whether the features that matter to one user overlap with those
that matter to another. They often need not. Consequently, removing the same 80% for everyone would be no
better than forcing the same 100% on everyone.
Unused capacity can also be deliberate. A rarely invoked emergency action, advanced diagnostic, or annual
reporting function may be valuable despite low frequency. Therefore the right optimization target is relevance,
task success, and realized value, not maximal feature adoption.

1.2 Forced upgrades impose unmeasured costs
A vendor may measure a new interface’s speed or adoption but overlook the transition costs borne by users
and organizations: relearning spatial routines, loss of familiarity, documentation and training changes, broken
automations, increased support requests, disturbed production work, or the effort of navigating unfamiliar set‐
tings. Changes can be welcome to some users and harmful to others.
At the organizational level, a parallel effect occurs when operating procedures are constantly redesigned. Even
beneficial changes require absorption capacity. People, agents, suppliers, integrations, and customers are all
parts of an operating system. Change has a cost; stability has value.

1.3 The opportunity is separation, not unlimited configurability
Liquid Software separates capability, context, interface, and evolution decision. It does not imply a screen that
rearranges itself unpredictably whenever a model sees a new pattern. A personalized workspace must be able
to stay familiar, remain inspectable, and support user‐controlled change.
Existing UX ideas such as progressive disclosure and interface customization already make important contribu‐
tions [R8, R9]. The broader proposal is to take the separation deeper: capabilities can originate from multiple
systems, be used directly by agents, and appear in different compositions without requiring the same vendor‐
imposed screen, application, or upgrade path.

2.1 Review layers are not merely a hierarchy of summaries
A daily review may identify a customer support backlog. A monthly review may infer a structural service‐capacity
problem. A quarterly review may conclude that the value proposition has drifted or that a segment needs a
different delivery model. These are different reasoning tasks using overlapping evidence, not three summaries
of the same dashboard.
Each review layer should define: purpose; scope; input evidence; required depth; authority; expected outputs;
default cadence; exceptional triggers; and criteria for revisiting its conclusions.

2.2 Persist understanding, not just reports
A recurring monthly review should not repeatedly reconstruct the entire organization from raw transactions and
conversations. Current operational state and derivations can be maintained continuously, while preserving the
original sources for verification and alternative interpretations.
A useful model distinguishes evidence, propositions, beliefs or assessments, and decisions. An assessment
should carry its supporting evidence, assumptions, confidence, dependencies, applicable scope, and last‐review
context. Its validity can be reconsidered when the underlying evidence changes.
This is consistent with the previously developed PIOS/EIOS approach: preserve provenance, distinguish canoni‐
cal from derived information, and retain a trace from original signals to decisions. Crucially, a previous conclusion
is not the same as a permanently correct fact.


3. Meta‐learning: the loops themselves must be reviewed
Above operational and strategic reviews sits a meta‐learning loop. It asks whether the organization’s own atten‐
tion, review rhythms, information processing, decision thresholds, and change practices produce good outcomes.
Its questions include:

    • Which important external signals are we missing or noticing too late?
    • Where do repeated reviews consume resources but rarely change a decision?
    • Which decisions are frequently reversed because assumptions were weak?
    • Are there profitable opportunities that our approval cadence delays unnecessarily?
    • Does excessive change reduce throughput, reliability, or customer confidence?
    • Are stable configurations being preserved long after external conditions make them uncompetitive?
    • Do experiments produce actionable evidence, or merely more dashboards?

The goal is not to maximize review frequency. It is to allocate attention and decision‐making effort where they
improve long‐term outcomes.

3.1 Scheduled and triggered reviews complement each other
A default review schedule supplies predictability. A triggering policy accelerates reconsideration when a thresh‐
old is crossed, a material dependency becomes invalid, an urgent customer issue recurs, or a market signal threat‐
ens strategic assumptions. A trigger should not automatically imply a change: it authorizes or requests review,
after which the answer may be to retain the current approach.
Triggers themselves should be assessed for false positives, missed events, delay, cost, and consequences. The
meta‐loop can recommend new frequencies or thresholds, but changes to governance rules still need an appro‐
priate authority.

3.2 Learning speed and change speed can be independently optimized
A system may observe continuously, recalculate a reliable metric nightly, deeply reflect quarterly, and deploy
a change only when evidence and transition readiness justify it. These distinct speeds are not a contradiction.
They are a desirable design property.


4. Stability enters the change equation
A common optimization mistake is to compare only the performance of an old and a proposed solution. The
actual choice is between future states including their transition paths.
A practical, qualitative decision model is:
       Expected realized improvement = prospective customer and business benefit ‐ implementation and
       transition costs ‐ disruption and switching costs ‐ added operational risk ‐ ongoing parallel‐support
       cost.
This is a decision heuristic, not a universal numeric equation. Its value lies in forcing explicit consideration of
costs that feature roadmaps and AI optimizers often omit.

4.1 Two top‐level outcome dimensions
Internal throughput: how effectively the organization turns time, capital, skills, information, and other resources
into valuable outputs, while maintaining quality and reliability.
External fitness: how well those outputs and the organization’s capabilities match changing customers, competi‐
tors, regulation, technology, and market conditions.
A business may efficiently produce an increasingly irrelevant offering. Another may correctly anticipate market
change but disrupt profitable operations through premature restructuring. Successful evolution requires both
dimensions and a realistic transition strategy.

4.2 Change is not binary
A review can reasonably recommend any of the following:

   1. Keep the current version, with no new action.
   2. Continue observation until evidence or relevance improves.
   3. Improve beneath the interface without changing established user workflows.
   4. Create and test an alternative without affecting production.
   5. Offer another version in parallel for a segment, context, or willing user.
   6. Adopt in stages with measurement and rollback paths.
   7. Retire an older version only after compatibility, user, legal, and support dependencies have been addressed.

The important design choice is to make these distinct outcomes first‐class. “Do nothing for now” may be the
correct, actively evaluated result.

4.3 Reversibility affects required evidence
A reversible interface layout trial can tolerate more experimentation than a contract change, major organizational
restructuring, or irreversible data migration. Expected impact, reversibility, and decision authority should influ‐
ence the approval gate. High‐disruption changes may merit staged pilots, explicit sign‐off, stronger evidence, or
delayed adoption even when the expected upside is substantial.


5. Parallel versions and deliberate retirement
Build new alongside what works; do not make replacement the default. Parallel versions are established in soft‐
ware delivery through blue‐green deployments, canary releases, feature toggles, and the parallel‐change pattern
[R10, R11, R12]. Liquid Software generalizes the principle beyond code to interfaces, Skills, agent workflows,
operating processes, and, where feasible, business models.

5.1 A version has more than a version number
A robust version record includes: capability or process identity; applicable CRD; implementation type; in‐
put/output contracts; supported contexts; dependencies; security scope; validation record; cost and latency;
migration compatibility; ownership; lifecycle status; and retirement conditions.
Useful statuses include experimental, candidate, active, active for a defined cohort, legacy‐supported, and
retired. More than one implementation can legitimately be active at once.

5.2 Independence between release and adoption
Publishing a new capability is not the same as forcing a new experience. A provider might make an implementa‐
tion available while an organization, user, or agent continues using a previous version. The separation only works
when contract compatibility, security updates, entitlement, data formats, and support obligations are designed
into the system.
Parallelism is not free. Supporting many versions can create a compatibility matrix, maintenance overhead, secu‐
rity exposure, and operational complexity. Retirement should therefore also be evidence‐based, not endlessly
postponed. Some emergency security, regulatory, or safety changes cannot remain purely optional.

5.3 Different realizations can coexist indefinitely
One CRD capability might be implemented through a large model, a smaller model, a deterministic rule engine,
conventional code, or a hybrid. An enterprise may prefer determinism for a stable, regulated workflow and
model reasoning for genuine exceptions. Another user may prefer a fully conversational experience for the
same underlying purpose. Multiple valid implementations are a feature of the architecture, not automatically
technical debt.


6. Liquid interfaces: personal, contextual, and stable
A user’s interface should reflect their work rather than a vendor’s full catalog of features. This includes which
capabilities appear, how much information is visible, which controls are direct, and whether an interface is
necessary at all.

6.2 Individual feature subsets are different, not merely smaller
One user benefits from schedule planning and recurring tasks; another needs dashboards and advanced filtering;
a third primarily uses search and export. The common capability inventory can be large while each personal
composition stays deliberately narrow. Removing features globally is therefore not the solution to low aggregate
adoption.

6.3 Personalized does not mean uncontrolled surveillance or manipulation
Personalization should respect owner‐controlled preferences, access permissions, purpose limitation, and the
ability to override inferred choices. Usage observations may inform recommendations, but should not silently
override an established workspace or create commercial upsell pressure disguised as optimization.
The person should be able to retain, preview, accept, reject, revert, and combine experiences. A useful design
default is suggest, do not impose, unless an authorized security or critical operational policy requires otherwise.

6.4 A stable experience can sit on rapidly evolving capabilities
A provider can improve a capability behind a durable interaction contract while the user’s established interface
remains unchanged. Conversely, a user can replace or recombine their interface without requiring the provider
to rewrite the underlying service. This independence is central to Liquid Software and is supported at the speci‐
fication level by CRD [R2].


7. CRD, Skills, agents, software, and frozen reasoning
The Capability Requirements Document (CRD) framework describes the smallest complete, contextually mean‐
ingful capability independently of the current technology. Its core includes purpose, boundaries, requirements,
interaction contracts, realizations, evidence, and unknowns [R2]. The original Liquid Software resource remains
the umbrella for choosing agent‐only, software‐only, or hybrid execution [R1].

7.1 A dedicated Reasoning Compilation Skill
One plausible reusable capability is a Reasoning Compilation Skill (or Crystallization Skill), whose purpose is to
identify repeatable portions of agent reasoning and turn them into explicit, testable implementations.
Its proposed lifecycle is:

   1. Inspect the Skill, CRD, operating constraints, examples, and execution evidence.
   2. Identify reasoning steps whose inputs, rules, and outputs can be formalized.
   3. Generate an implementation using code, a rule engine, or a reasoning‐block technology.
   4. Validate against the CRD, independent tests, invariants, and adverse cases.
   5. Register a candidate version with provenance, assumptions, and limitations.
   6. Test in shadow or parallel operation; promote only with required approval.
   7. Monitor drift, dependency changes, exceptions, and new versions of the capability.

Importantly, such a Skill should have its own CRD and governance rules. Automated compilation is a proposed
design pattern, not a confirmed capability of a particular vendor.

7.2 What can and cannot be frozen
Good candidates: repeatable calculations; explicit allocation and routing rules; permission checks derived
from authoritative policies; validation of input completeness; stable constraint optimization; and deterministic
business‐rule execution.
Partial candidates: document interpretation followed by rule‐based validation; agentic workflow orchestration
with deterministic gates; or analytical Skills with stable numeric components and open‐ended interpretation.
Poor candidates: ambiguous judgment with evolving context; strategic sense‐making; unverified assumptions;
interpretation of rapidly changing laws without authoritative updates; and personalized advice where the rele‐
vant goals are unresolved.
Freezing a decision path does not make its assumptions true, its evidence fresh, or its implementation correct. A
deterministic program can consistently reproduce the wrong answer. Validation, versioning, source dependen‐
cies, and invalidation are mandatory if frozen logic is to be trusted.

7.3 MDIL and Optivian illustrate different reuse opportunities
MDIL Kide and REMAP present a declared‐reasoning and deterministic agent‐decision approach. Their pub‐
lished materials emphasize readable rules, auditable decisions, and moving portions of agent control outside
generation [R5]. This is relevant to executable reasoning and guarded routing. A commercial product’s sup‐
ported interfaces, proof level, openness, and implementation details must be evaluated directly; it would be
incorrect to assume automatic CRD compliance or generalized correctness.
Optivian presents an adjacent but distinct optimization: process source interactions when they arrive, keep an
updated structured view, and retrieve focused evidence rather than repeatedly reconstructing the same context
from raw CRM, email, and meeting records. In a vendor‐reported example, billable tokens declined from 933,294
to 158,691 (roughly 5.9x less), with estimated per‐run cost declining from $2.96 to $0.53 [R6]. This is a reported
customer example, not a universal independently measured benchmark.

8. Pulse CXM: outside‐in intelligence and verified improvement
Pulse CXM is an AI‐native customer experience management approach associated with Digiole, designed to turn
customer interaction signals into coordinated cross‐team action and verified improvement [R3]. It should not
become a generic replacement for all organizational intelligence. Its distinctive role is to connect customer‐lived
experience with the organization’s ability to change and confirm value.
The established four‐part operating model is:

   1. Pulse: collect source‐linked signals, interactions, contextual information, recurring issues, and emerging
      patterns.
   2. Actions: identify opportunities and problems; prioritize them; assign responsibility; account for dependen‐
      cies and organizational objectives.
   3. Execution: carry out cross‐team work, experiments, changes, and phased adoption.
   4. Conclusions: verify what happened, whether the original issue recurred, whether outcomes improved, and
      what management should conclude.

Completion is not resolution. An issue is not resolved simply because a task was closed, a feature shipped, or
a new interface released. It is resolved to the extent that the customer problem or opportunity changes in the
intended way.

8.1 Listening by helping
Talking Products and Talking Venues provide illustrative customer interaction sources: contextual, multilingual,
help‐first conversations that create a useful experience for the customer while generating authentic signals for
the organization [R4]. Pulse CXM can also use support interactions, reviews, sales, product telemetry, customer
conversations, and other legitimate sources.
The important shift is that customer insight becomes a continuous byproduct of service, not only a survey
campaign. Context and appropriate consent matter: the mere availability of a signal does not justify unlimited
reuse of personal data.

8.2 Pulse CXM feeds EIOS without swallowing EIOS
EIOS governs the organization’s broader objectives, resource choices, internal and external performance, knowl‐
edge continuity, and review architecture. Pulse CXM supplies an essential customer‐centered learning and exe‐
cution stream. Organization‐level DNA (mission, strategy, principles), Growth (OKRs, KPIs, teams, performance),
and Settings (sources, reporting, configuration) help contextualize Pulse decisions, without redefining the four
operational stages.
Pulse CXM can influence reviews at several horizons: immediate service failures, recurring monthly friction,

8.3 Pulse can monitor unwanted change as well as insufficient change
A particularly powerful extension is to treat disruption caused by improvements as a customer‐experience signal.
Pulse CXM can track whether a redesigned interface creates new confusion, whether expert users lose essential
shortcuts, whether a forced change generates support traffic, or whether a minority segment gains substantial
value from an optional alternative.
This makes Pulse CXM relevant not only to what to improve but also to how to release, adopt, and retire im‐
provements. It can compare experience cohorts without presuming that the newest version should prevail.

9.1 The decision sequence
For each candidate improvement, the system should be able to answer:
Check: What triggered the review? Which evidence is new?
Why: Which internal‐throughput or external‐fitness outcome matters?
What: What capability, interface, process, assumption, or policy might change?
Who: Who benefits, who is disrupted, and who has authority?

How: Retain, adjust, experiment, parallel release, opt‐in, migrate, or retire?

9.2 Governance and fallback
The framework should never let a predictive suggestion silently become an authorized policy. Distinguish ob‐
served fact, derived inference, proposed change, approved decision, and deployed implementation. Preserve
who or what made the decision and under which authority.
For user interfaces, personal preferences and overrides are part of the authority model. For organizational pro‐
cesses, authorization can depend on financial exposure, legal consequences, customer impact, and rollback fea‐
sibility. For frozen logic, changed inputs or assumptions may trigger revalidation rather than blind reuse.


10. Worked scenarios
Scenario A: A software interface users experience differently
Signals. Pulse CXM detects recurring complaints that a business interface is distracting. Usage data reveals that
a separate expert cohort frequently opens detailed panels and wants more simultaneous information. The two
needs are not contradictory.
Review. The product capability inventory and CRDs show that underlying capabilities can remain shared. A
monthly CX review proposes two interface compositions rather than one forced redesign. A strategic review
asks whether these cohorts represent durable market segments.
Evolution. The existing UI remains active. A focused workspace and an expert workspace are tested with
voluntary cohorts. A hybrid composition is available for tasks combining the two perspectives. Changes can be
previewed and reverted.
Validation. Evaluate time‐to‐complete, error rates, task success, unsolicited complaints, continuity, accessibility,
and whether the same person uses different modes by context. Pulse Conclusions tests whether the friction
actually declines; usage alone does not establish that.
Resulting principle. One shared capability layer can support several legitimate experiences; adoption is contex‐
tual and should not require unnecessary migration.

Scenario B: A recurring operational workflow with frozen reasoning
Signals. EIOS identifies a repeatable procurement process with expensive agent reasoning, intermittent mistakes,
and a stable set of rules.
Review and specification. A CRD clarifies required checks, exception handling, approvals, and outputs. A Rea‐
soning Compilation Skill identifies steps suitable for deterministic execution. The original model‐driven imple‐
mentation remains available for unresolved exceptions.
Parallel validation. The new implementation runs in shadow mode using real or representative inputs without
making duplicate side effects. Nightly tests verify constraints, policy changes, output quality, and dependency
versions. The system compares latency, cost, accuracy, failure modes, and auditability.
Adoption. A permitted subset of requests moves to the new implementation. The older execution path contin‐
ues for cases outside the validated scope and as a rollback option. Retirement occurs only when coverage and
support economics justify it.
Resulting principle. Freeze formalizable reasoning, not the organization’s entire capacity to think or the truth of
changing evidence.

Scenario C: A market shift before the next quarterly review
Signals. A competitor introduces a business model that changes customer expectations. Customer conversa‐
tions, lost deals, and market data begin to align.
Review. An event‐triggered strategic review starts ahead of the scheduled quarter. The review tests competing
explanations, considers the effect on market position, and assesses whether the signal is temporary or structural.
Evolution. The organization pilots a new offer for one customer segment while maintaining the current business
for others. A new capability or pricing process can operate in parallel; the transition is not a forced replacement.
Validation. Measure retention, margins, conversion, delivery capacity, and operational disruption, then compare
the evidence at the next scheduled strategic review. The meta‐loop considers whether trigger sensitivity was
appropriate.
Resulting principle. Review can accelerate without requiring premature strategic change.

Scenario D: Personal Core and organizational Core
The pattern has a natural owner boundary. PIOS can maintain an individual’s preferred workspaces, task rhythms,
permissions, trusted facts, and personally derived knowledge. EIOS can maintain organizational policies, capabil‐
ity versions, review records, and cross‐team outcomes. Neither should assume unrestricted access to the other’s
context.
A person can use an EIOS‐backed capability in a personally composed interface without surrendering personal
history or UI preferences to the organization. Sharing occurs through authorized interfaces, and organizational
agents do not automatically gain access to an individual’s Core.

11.1 Research questions still open
    • How should different review layers share evidence without hard‐wiring a rigid reporting hierarchy?
    • What thresholds justify a triggered strategic review rather than monitoring alone?
    • How do we quantify the positive value of stable workflows and familiar interfaces?
    • When does supporting multiple active versions cost more than the personalization benefit?
    • What is the smallest interoperable metadata standard for an experience composition?
    • Can frozen reasoning remain portable across runtimes and providers, with verifiable semantics?
    • How should an AI‐native organization distinguish reversible experimentation from changes that require
      accountable human approval?
    • Which signals show that customer experience problems have truly disappeared, rather than merely chang‐
      ing form?


12. Design principles and scope boundaries
   1. Optimize realized outcomes, not change frequency. The objective combines internal throughput, external
      fitness, and transition effects.
   2. Separate sensing, understanding, deciding, adopting, and verifying. Different rhythms and authorities are


Continuous intelligence. Adaptive evolution. Stable experience.                                                           15

Original PDF page 17

LIQUID SOFTWARE / COMPANION RESOURCE                                                                  28 NOV 2026



     appropriate.
  3. Give stability explicit value. Familiarity and production continuity belong in the decision, not as
     afterthoughts.
  4. Use fixed cycles as defaults and triggers as exceptions. The meta‐loop learns whether both are effective.
  5. Preserve canonical evidence and traceable derivations. Previously generated knowledge can be reused
     without becoming unquestioned truth.
  6. Specify capabilities independently of implementations. CRD meaning should outlive a particular Skill,
     model, UI, or vendor.
  7. Compile only what can be formally checked. Determinism is not a substitute for correctness or fresh
     evidence.
  8. Separate release from adoption. A new version need not replace an older one for everybody.
  9. Support multiple legitimate experiences. Individual, task, and context preferences are not merely maturity
     levels.
 10. Treat retirement as a decision. Parallel versions have maintenance costs and cannot proliferate indefinitely.
 11. Measure customer resolution, not task completion. Pulse CXM connects changes to lived outcomes.
 12. Keep authority, consent, and ownership explicit. Personalized interfaces and organizational optimizers
     should not become instruments of forced change.

Non‐goals: an interface that continually rearranges itself; automatic production changes from unreviewed agent
conclusions; a requirement to turn every function into a CRD; a belief that all reasoning can be frozen; an as‐
sumption that all users should eventually converge on the same expert UI; and a claim that any single vendor
currently offers this complete architecture.


13. Relationship to the wider Liquid Software resource
The umbrella Liquid Software concept asks whether a task needs software at all, whether an agent can perform
it directly, and whether the appropriate solution is a dynamic combination of agents, existing software, newly
generated software, and user‐facing interfaces [R1].
This companion paper adds the temporal and governance dimension:
      What should the system check, why, how deeply, and when? If evidence supports a change, how
      should it be developed, tested, introduced, adopted, or retired while preserving what already works?
Its distinct contribution is to unite organizational review cycles, stability‐aware change economics, parallel im‐
plementation and UX versioning, CRD‐guided validation, reusable reasoning, and customer‐led outcome ver‐
ification. These principles are relevant beyond any particular PIOS/EIOS instance or Pulse CXM deployment,
while those systems provide concrete application contexts.
The architecture therefore links three independent rates: the rate of sensing, the rate of learning, and the rate
of change. Liquid Software seeks to improve each without assuming they should be synchronized.
