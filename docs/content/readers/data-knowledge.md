# Liquid Software for data and knowledge

Liquid Software gives source information a life beyond the application or first interpretation that uses it. For data stewards, knowledge workers and people managing their own information, the main question is **what should we preserve, and which processing should we invest in as needs emerge?**

The resource separates appropriate source retention from summaries, extraction, indexing and presentation. Each has value, cost and governing obligations.

## Preserve evidence for questions you cannot yet name

A summary of a meeting may answer today's question while omitting evidence needed later. An appropriate original can preserve possibilities for reinterpretation. Structured records can themselves be authoritative; the issue is losing relevant evidence by keeping only anticipated fields.

Preservation remains bounded by purpose, consent, ownership, retention and resources. Capture for Unknown Future Value is not an instruction to collect everything. No source captures all reality, and richer evidence still has interpretive limits.

## Keep originals, assertions and indexes distinguishable

An observation, an accepted assertion, an inferred interpretation and a derived retrieval structure serve different purposes. A faster index should not silently become the authority for facts it was built to serve. Changing a model or retrieval method should preserve source references, freshness and uncertainty.

For personal information, the same question applies across notes, files and conversations: what stays understandable if the current tool disappears? The architecture's principal and information concerns help frame this question without making every related information framework a required dependency.

## Invest in retrieval around demand and commitments

Processing on retrieval, selective caching and broader preprocessing are alternative strategies. Compare complete costs and the outcome they support. Adequate discovery, metadata, access control and accessibility may be needed before observed demand can be learned. Freshness and response-time commitments can justify upfront preparation.

In v3's illustrative example, equivalent basic indexing and access are already available. Full extraction and horizon upkeep cost €1,300, followed by €0.01 per retrieval; on-demand extraction costs €0.20 per retrieval. At 1,000 retrievals the totals are €1,310 and €200; at 10,000 they are €1,400 and €2,000. Preprocessing first costs less at 6,843 whole retrievals.

These are explanatory assumptions. Retrieval events are different from the archive's document count. Repeated access to a few documents may justify selective caching rather than extracting the entire archive.

## Examine a changing question

Imagine a team initially asking for dates from a document collection, then asking which decisions changed and why. Preserved originals can support the second inquiry; a date-only index cannot reconstruct discarded reasoning. New interpretations should remain attributable to their sources and expose gaps instead of inventing certainty.

Retiring a derived optimization does not authorize source deletion. Preservation, revision history and recovery deserve their own decision, including any obligations that require eventual deletion. Adaptive presentation also needs to preserve evidence when a table becomes a narrative or a spoken answer.

## Questions to explore

- Which evidence would be lost if only today's summary or fields survived?
- Which sources are appropriate to retain, for whom and for how long?
- What preparation is necessary before safe and useful retrieval?
- What demand, freshness or latency evidence justifies another derived structure?
